# The VillageWatch Coordinator Guide

Everything you need to run VillageWatch for your village. No technical
knowledge is assumed. If you can run a parish noticeboard and a WhatsApp group,
you can run this.

Read it once end to end before you open the village to residents. After that,
treat it as a reference — the section headings above are links.

---

## Welcome

### What VillageWatch is

VillageWatch is a way for the people who live in your village to tell each other
what they have seen, without any of them having to put their name, their
neighbour's name or a car registration in front of the whole village.

It lives at **villagewatch.app**. There is nothing to install — a resident opens
that address in the browser they already use, and their phone will offer to add
it to the home screen, after which it behaves like any other app.

A resident opens the app and describes what happened in their own words. Before
anything is saved, the description is rewritten to take out the details that
identify people — names, registrations, house numbers, descriptions that only
fit one person. The resident reads that rewrite and either accepts it or edits
it. Only then is the report filed.

If they attach a photo, faces in it are covered on their own phone, before the
picture is sent anywhere. The original never leaves the device.

The location is deliberately imprecise. Every pin on the map is moved a short
distance from the place that was actually reported, so a pin can tell you which
end of the lane something happened at and cannot tell you which house.

What the village then gets is a map, a list of recent reports, and a phone
alert when something serious is published.

### What being a coordinator means

You are the person who decides what the village sees. In data protection terms
somebody has to be the *data controller* — the person or body legally answerable
for all of this — and which one you are depends on how your village is run.

**Most villages have no parish council behind them.** In that case **you** are
the controller. It is not a title you apply for; it is a description of what you
are already doing, and it would be true whether or not anybody wrote it down. The
Community Coordinator Agreement in the next section sets out what it obliges you
to do, and the compliance screen lists the three duties with a deadline on them
before it asks you to accept anything.

**Where a parish or town council has taken the village on**, the council is the
controller and you act on its behalf.

Either way VillageWatch is the *processor*: we run the software and do what the
controller tells us.

That relationship is the reason for the paperwork in the next section, and it is
worth understanding rather than clicking past. When a resident asks who holds
their data, the answer is you or the council. When something goes wrong, that is
also who the Information Commissioner writes to.

### What you can do

- See every report filed in your village, including ones waiting for review and
  ones that were rejected.
- Publish a report to the village, or reject it.
- Read a reporter's original wording — the words they typed before the rewrite.
  Every time you do this it is recorded.
- Mark a report resolved, or archive an old one.
- Change your village's settings: the council's name, how strongly faces are
  covered, your WhatsApp Channel, and whether reports are published
  automatically.
- Produce a report for your police contact or a council meeting, and download a
  spreadsheet of the village's reports.
- Read the village's audit trail — a record of what every coordinator has done.

### What you cannot do

- **See another village.** Every screen in the app shows your village and only
  your village. There is no view across villages and no way to ask for one.
- **Approve a resident's application to become a coordinator.** Those go to the
  platform administrator, not to you. See "Managing your community".
- **Change your own role, or anybody else's.** Roles are set by the system when
  an application is approved.
- **Change or delete the audit trail.** Nothing can, including us. That is the
  point of it.
- **Un-accept a compliance document, or move a village back off the council
  model.** Acceptances are a record of something that happened on a date, so
  nothing on screen can rewrite them.
- **Recover a published report.** Once a report is published, residents have
  seen it and may have shared it. Think of publishing as final.

## Getting started

Five things, in this order. The first one is not optional — until it is done
your village cannot accept a single report.

### 1. Accept the compliance documents

Go to **Compliance** in the sidebar, or Dashboard then Compliance.

What you see there depends on how your village is run, and most villages get the
first of these two.

#### If there is no parish council behind your village — the community model

You will find **one** document: the **Community Coordinator Agreement**. It is
written for a volunteer rather than for a clerk and takes about ten minutes.

It does two jobs. It is the contract between you and Yakasista Ltd, who run the
software — a controller may only use a processor under a written contract. And
it is the **policy document** the law requires before anyone may collect reports
about suspected crime: reports are *criminal offence data*, which is treated more
strictly than ordinary personal data, and processing it lawfully at all depends
on having that document in place. Without one there is no lawful authorisation —
not a paperwork gap, an actual absence of legal basis.

**You are the data controller.** That is a description of what you are already
doing rather than a title you apply for: you decide who is invited in, what gets
published and what happens to a report. Three duties come with it and the screen
sets them out above the agreement — answering a resident's request within one
month, reporting a serious breach within 72 hours, and keeping a record of what
the village processes. Sections 2 to 5 of the agreement are that record for
everything the software does, and info@yakasista.com is where to go when one of
the other two actually happens.

One signature, and it is in force when you accept it. There is no paper copy
waiting on a countersignature.

#### If a parish or town council runs your village — the council model

You will find **three** documents, shown in full. They are written for a parish
councillor and between them run to a good half hour — put the kettle on.

- **The Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA).** What the service does with
  residents' information, what could go wrong, and what has been built to stop
  it. UK GDPR Article 35 requires this before high-risk processing starts, not
  after.
- **The Appropriate Policy Document (APD).** The same policy document described
  above, as a document of the council's own — which is what a council is
  separately obliged to hold.
- **The Data Processing Agreement (DPA).** The contract between your council and
  Yakasista Ltd. A council using VillageWatch without one is in breach from the
  first report filed.

Tick all three and press **Accept and enable village**. Your name and the date
are recorded against each one, and each is written to the audit trail
separately.

#### Either way

- **This blocks reporting until it is done.** A resident who opens the report
  form before you have finished this screen is told the village is not accepting
  reports yet and given the 999 and 101 numbers. That is deliberate.
- **It cannot be undone from this screen.** A controller that adopted a document
  on a date did adopt it on that date. If you or the council later want to
  withdraw, that is a conversation with us, not a checkbox.
- **The council's processing agreement takes two signatures and you would only
  be providing one.** Ticking that box records the council's acceptance of the
  terms; the agreement is not in force until Yakasista Ltd has signed the paper
  copy too. Send the signed copy to info@yakasista.com and keep the
  countersigned version with the council's records. The community agreement is
  not like this — it takes one signature and is in force when you accept it.

Take your own advice on the terms before accepting, and if a council has its own
standard processing agreement, use that instead — tell us and we will sign it.

#### If a council takes the village on later

At the foot of the compliance screen there is a control for exactly that. It
switches the village to the council model, and the council then adopts the three
documents above and becomes the data controller from the date it does.

Two things worth knowing. **Your village does not close while that happens** —
reporting carries on under the agreement you accepted, because you are still the
controller until the council has adopted its own, and nobody should have to wait
for a parish meeting to file a report. And **your acceptance is not deleted**:
you were the controller for that period, and the record of it is what shows the
handover was orderly. It is one-way, so if a council later steps back, talk to us
rather than switching it back.

### 2. Set the name of whoever is answerable

Go to **Dashboard** and find the parish council field in Village Settings.

If a council runs your village, type its full legal name exactly as it appears
on its own letterhead — "Histon and Impington Parish Council", not "Histon PC".
There is no fixed format, because the real names are not uniform: parish
meetings, town councils and Welsh community councils all differ.

If no council runs your village, put the name people would recognise as the
group — "Histon Neighbourhood Watch", or your own name if that is what the
village would recognise. The field is free text and is not validated against
anything.

This name is printed at the foot of every document the app produces for your
police contact or the council, and it is who a resident is told to take a data
protection complaint to. Get it wrong and a subject access request goes somewhere
with no authority to answer it.

Do this before the first report is filed, not after.

### 3. Choose how strongly faces are covered

Also on the Dashboard. Four options, and there is a small preview of each one
next to it.

| Level | What it does | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light blur | The softest option. Keeps the most of the scene around the face. | Only if your police contact has specifically asked for it. |
| Standard blur | The default. Faces unreadable; you can still see how many people there were and where they stood. | Almost every village, almost all of the time. |
| Heavy blur | A wider smear that also takes hair, build and clothing at the collar. | A village that has had a complaint, or one where people know each other well enough to recognise a coat. |
| Full redact | A solid black rectangle over the face. Nothing of it is left in the file. | The most cautious setting, and the one to choose if you are unsure. |

Two things about this that are easy to misread:

- **Every level covers every face.** The scale does not decide *whether* a face
  is hidden — it decides how much of the surroundings go with it. Even the
  lightest setting destroys the face itself; you cannot accidentally set this to
  "leave people recognisable".
- **It applies to photos added after you change it.** A photo a resident has
  already attached keeps the treatment it was given when it was processed. The
  reporter's screen says so.

A reporter can always ask for *more* covering than you have set — there is a
checkbox on the upload that blacks a face out completely. There is deliberately
no way for a reporter to ask for less.

If you have a police contact, ask them what they would prefer before you choose.
Some forces would rather have a lightly blurred image they can work with; most
would rather you did not collect the photograph at all.

### 4. Set up your WhatsApp Channel

Optional, and most villages want only the first half of this.

A WhatsApp Channel is the broadcast kind — you post, followers read, nobody can
reply and followers cannot see each other. It is not a group chat. If your
village does not have one, you create it inside WhatsApp itself in about a
minute.

Once you have one, open WhatsApp, go to the channel, and copy its invite link.
Paste that link into the WhatsApp Channel field on the Dashboard. That is all
you have to do — the app works the rest out from the link.

What that gets you:

- Every resident's Settings screen gains a **Follow on WhatsApp** button, so
  people can find the channel without you posting the link again every month.
- When you approve a report, a ready-made alert appears with a **Copy to
  WhatsApp** button next to it. Press it, open the channel, paste.

**Nothing is posted automatically, ever.** There is no robot with access to your
channel. Every message that appears there was pasted by a person who had just
read the report. This is a deliberate design decision, not a missing feature:
the channel is the one place where what you post can be read by someone who is
not a resident of your village, and we would rather a human made that call every
time.

There are two switches beside the field. One turns channel alerts on for the
village, and one sets the lowest severity worth an alert — it starts at High,
which is stricter than the phone alerts residents get, because a channel is
public and phone alerts are not.

### 5. Decide about auto-approve

The last setting, and the biggest one. It is **off** by default.

With auto-approve **off**, every report a resident files waits in a queue until
you look at it. You get a phone alert when something arrives. Nothing reaches
the village map until you press Approve.

With auto-approve **on**, reports are published the moment they are filed. The
phone alert goes out, the pin appears on the map, and the first you know about
it is the same time as everybody else.

Turning it on removes the only person standing between a resident's submission
and the whole village. The rewrite that takes out identifying details is good,
but it is not moderation — it cannot tell that a report is a neighbour dispute,
or malicious, or about a child.

Reasonable villages choose either. Turn it on if:

- your village is small and you know everybody who has joined;
- reports are rare enough that a day in the queue is a real delay; and
- you have read what a report looks like when the rewrite did not run, which is
  the case that catches people out — when the rewrite is unavailable, what gets
  published is the reporter's own words.

Keep it off if you are new to this, if the village is large, or if you have any
reason to expect reports about identifiable individuals.

Two more things:

- **Turning it on does not empty the queue.** Reports already waiting were filed
  under a promise that somebody would look at them, and some of them are sitting
  there because you had doubts. The queue stays; the setting applies to what is
  filed next.
- **Auto-approve and WhatsApp Channel posting together are a big step.** It
  means an unreviewed report is one paste away from the open internet. Nothing
  stops you doing it, and you should do it knowingly. If you file a report
  yourself in an auto-approving village, you land on a screen with the alert
  already on it, having just read what it says.

Every one of these five settings is recorded in the audit trail with your name
against it.

## Managing incidents

### The moderation queue

If auto-approve is off, this is the daily work. It is on the Dashboard, under
the figures.

Each report in the queue shows you the title, the anonymised description, the
type and severity, when and roughly where it happened, and any photo. That is
enough to make the decision in most cases.

Read it as a resident would. The question is not "is this true" — you often
cannot know — but "should the village see this, in these words".

### Approving

Press **Approve**. Four things happen at once:

1. The report appears on the village map and in the incident list, and stays
   there for twelve months.
2. A phone alert goes out to residents who have notifications on, whose severity
   threshold this report meets, and who live close enough to it. Anybody whose
   home location is unknown gets the alert — a distance filter is a way to hear
   less, not a reason to be silently left out.
3. The reporter is told their report was published.
4. If your village has a WhatsApp Channel set up, an alert appears on screen
   with a copy button, ready to paste.

An entry goes in the audit trail with your name on it.

Approving is not reversible. You can archive a report afterwards, which takes it
off the map, but the alert has already gone out and residents have already read
it.

### Rejecting

Press **Reject**, with a note.

The note is not optional, and it is not for you — it is quoted back to the
reporter. A rejection with nothing after it tells a volunteer nothing about
whether they should have reported it, whether to try again, or whether they have
done something wrong. "Please report this to 101 first — we can add it here once
you have a crime number" is a note. "Rejected" is not.

Reject when:

- **It is an emergency.** Someone is in danger, or a crime is happening now.
  Reject it and tell them to call 999. The app is not a way of contacting the
  police.
- **It is a dispute between two neighbours.** These are almost never improved by
  publication, and the village map is the worst possible venue for one.
- **It identifies somebody anyway.** The rewrite catches names and
  registrations. It cannot catch "the man at the house with the blue door", and
  in a village of four hundred people that is an identification.
- **It is speculation presented as fact.** "A silver car went past twice" is a
  report. "There's a gang casing the village" is not.
- **It is about a child**, unless it plainly needs to be there.
- **It is not a community safety matter at all** — a planning grievance, a
  parking row, a dog.

Rejecting does not delete the report. It stays in the system, visible to you and
to the reporter, and out of sight of everybody else. If you want it gone
entirely, the reporter can delete it, and so can you.

### Resolving and archiving

A published report can be marked **Resolved** when the matter is closed — the
bike was found, the police attended, the fly-tipping was cleared. It stays
visible, marked as resolved. This is worth doing: a map full of unresolved
reports reads as a village where nothing ever gets sorted out.

**Archive** takes a report off the map and out of the list. Use it for something
that has stopped being useful — a report about a road closure that has since
reopened, or a duplicate of another report.

You do not have to archive things to keep the map tidy. Published reports are
archived automatically after twelve months, and photographs are deleted after
six, whether or not anybody touches them.

### Reading the reporter's original wording

In the queue there is a button marked **Show the reporter's original wording**.
It shows you what the resident actually typed, before the identifying details
were taken out.

Use it sparingly, and only when the decision in front of you genuinely turns on
it. Reasonable uses: the anonymised version is incoherent and you cannot tell
what happened; you suspect the report is malicious; the police have asked a
specific question.

**Every single press is recorded** — your name, the report, the time — and other
coordinators in your village can see that record on the audit trail, as can we.
That is not a threat, it is the design. The reporter's own words are the most
sensitive thing in the system, and the only thing that makes it safe to let
anybody read them is that nobody can read them quietly.

Never copy those words anywhere. Not into an email, not into WhatsApp, not into
a text to your PCSO. See "Privacy responsibilities".

## Sharing with police and the council

Everything in this section carries the anonymised description. None of it
carries the reporter's original wording, the reporter's identity, home
addresses, or exact coordinates. That is a property of how the documents are
built, not something you have to remember to check.

### One report

Open a published report and press **Share with police**. Your phone or computer
offers its usual share options — email, message, whatever you use. If sharing is
not available, the summary is copied to your clipboard instead.

Available on published reports only. If you want your police contact to have a
report that is still in the queue, approve it first. That is a decision, and it
leaves a record.

The summary includes a link back to the report, which needs a signed-in resident
of your village to open. Your PCSO will not be able to follow it unless they are
one. That is intentional; the summary itself carries what they need.

### A period report

Go to **Reports** in the sidebar. Choose a date range — the last week and the
last month are the usual ones — and you get a document covering every report
published in that period: how many, of what kind, how serious, where the
clusters are.

There is a button that writes a narrative summary of the period in prose,
suitable for reading out or forwarding. It is a separate button because it costs
something to produce, and because you should be able to change the dates half a
dozen times without spending it. If that summary is unavailable, the report
still works — it says plainly that the figures were assembled by counting rather
than written, so nobody mistakes an outage for a quiet month.

**Download PDF** gives you the report as a file, built by the service rather
than by your browser — so the copy you send this month looks like the one you
sent last month, whatever device you are on. If you have written the narrative
summary, the PDF is written fresh as you download it, so its wording may differ
from the paragraph on screen; **Copy report** is the one that keeps exactly what
you are looking at.

Generating a period report is recorded in the audit trail. Producing a document
that leaves the village is the act worth recording; looking at the dashboard
figures is not.

### The spreadsheet

On the Dashboard, **Export CSV** downloads your village's reports as a
spreadsheet: reference, date, type, severity, status, the anonymised
description, the approximate location description.

This is the one for a council meeting — paste it into a spreadsheet, make a
chart of it, count the burglaries by quarter.

It deliberately does **not** contain the reporters' original wording. One report
read on screen with a record of the read is a controlled act; a spreadsheet is
emailed, forwarded and left on a laptop, and a name in one cannot be recalled.
Nor does it contain coordinates, reporter identities or home addresses.

Deleted reports are excluded. Treat the file as council information: keep it
where the council keeps its other papers, and do not forward it to a residents'
group.

## Managing your community

### How residents join

Your village has a join code — six characters, no easily confused letters,
designed to be read aloud down the phone or printed in a newsletter.

A resident goes to **villagewatch.app/register**, finds your village in the
list, and enters that code. It is the code that says they were invited by the
village rather than having simply found it.

Get the address and the code to residents the way you already reach them: the
parish newsletter, the noticeboard, the existing WhatsApp group, the annual
meeting. The address is public and can go anywhere. The code is not a secret in
the password sense — it is a way of keeping out somebody two counties away who
liked the look of the map — but do not post it on a public website.

If a code gets somewhere it should not, ask us to issue a new one. Everybody
already signed up stays signed up; only new joiners need the new code.

### Coordinator applications

A resident who wants to help moderate applies from their own Settings screen.
They give their standing in the village — parish councillor, Neighbourhood Watch
coordinator, and so on — and their reasons.

**You do not approve these.** They go to the platform administrator, who decides
and notifies the applicant. This is not a judgement about coordinators; it is
that the same screen has to work for a village that does not have a coordinator
yet, and a village-appointed reviewer could only ever be somebody who already
holds the role being applied for.

What you should do is the part the software cannot: know who is applying. If you
want somebody appointed, email info@yakasista.com and say so. A word from the
existing coordinator is the strongest thing an application can have behind it,
and it is much faster than waiting for us to work out who is who.

Two or three coordinators is a good number for a village. One is a single point
of failure — holidays, illness, moving away — and more than about four means
nobody feels responsible for the queue.

### Handling inappropriate reports

Most of what arrives is fine. For the rest:

- **Reject it with a note** that explains what would have made it acceptable.
  Most bad reports are a well-meaning resident misjudging the tone.
- **If a resident repeatedly files reports that identify people**, talk to them
  directly. This is a village; a conversation works better than a rejection
  note, and they are usually trying to be helpful.
- **If a report is about a specific named person and reads as harassment**,
  reject it and tell us. Do not leave it in the queue and do not discuss it in
  the village WhatsApp group.
- **If a resident asks you to take their own report down**, take it down. See
  the next section.
- **If somebody is using the app to threaten or target another resident**, that
  is a police matter and a matter for us. Contact both.

### When to contact us

Email info@yakasista.com for:

- A resident who should be made a coordinator, or one who should not be any
  more.
- A join code that needs replacing.
- A resident who wants their account closed and cannot do it themselves.
- A subject access request, or any letter from the Information Commissioner.
  Tell us the same day. There are statutory deadlines on these and they are
  short.
- Anything that looks like a data breach — an alert that went out with a name in
  it, a photograph where a face was not covered, a report visible to somebody
  who should not see it. Same day, and before you try to fix it yourself. We may
  have 72 hours to report it.
- Anything you are unsure about. There is no such thing as a silly question here
  and there is such a thing as a late one.

## Privacy responsibilities

This is the section to reread every few months.

### You are acting for the council

Everything you do in this app, you do on behalf of your parish or town council,
which is the data controller. You are not a moderator on a website; you are
handling other people's personal data under an authorisation the council holds.

Practically, that means: if you would not do it with a folder of paper reports
from your neighbours, do not do it here.

### Never take original wording out of the app

The single most important rule in this guide.

When you press the button to see a reporter's original words, those words are
for the decision in front of you. They do not go into an email to a councillor,
a WhatsApp message to a PCSO, a text to another coordinator, or a note in your
own files.

The anonymised version is the one that leaves the app. Everything the app builds
for sharing carries that version and cannot carry the other one. The only way
the original gets out is if a person copies it out, and you are that person.

### Do not screenshot and forward

A screenshot bypasses everything. The moderation queue on your screen shows
detail the village never sees, and a photograph of your screen in a WhatsApp
group is the same disclosure as publishing it, without the record.

Use the sharing buttons. They exist so that you never have to take a screenshot,
and they produce a document that is safe to send.

The same goes for the map with pins on it, and the spreadsheet.

### Respect a request to delete

A resident can delete their own report at any time, and close their account.
This is their right under UK GDPR Article 17 and it is not conditional on you
agreeing that it is reasonable.

If someone asks you to remove a report they filed, point them at the delete
button on their own report, or delete it for them. Do it promptly. If they want
their whole account gone, that is on their Settings screen.

Deleting is thorough — the report's contents are erased, including the original
wording, and any photograph is deleted from storage. What survives is an empty
record that the report once existed, because the audit trail refers to it and
the trail cannot be edited.

A published report that is deleted has still been seen. Do not promise a
resident that deletion unwinds publication, because it does not.

### Review the documents once a year

Whatever your village accepted is due for review a year after the date it was
accepted — the Community Coordinator Agreement, or the DPIA and the Appropriate
Policy Document. Put it in the diary now, or in the council's calendar; the same
meeting each year is easiest.

Reviewing means reading them again and asking whether they still describe what
is happening: has the village changed the privacy level, turned auto-approve on,
started posting to a WhatsApp Channel, grown by three hundred people. If the
answer is yes, the assessment needs to say so.

For a council that review is a council act recorded in its minutes and a fresh
signature on the paper document. For a community village it is you re-reading the
agreement and telling us if anything in it has stopped being true. Either way,
tell us and we will send updated copies.

## Tips and good practice

**Put the WhatsApp Channel link where people already are.** The channel is only
worth having if people follow it, and nobody joins a channel they have to search
for. Post the link in the existing village WhatsApp group, the Facebook group,
the newsletter and the noticeboard, and post it again in three months. Most
residents will follow the channel long before they install the app, and that is
fine — the channel is the reach and the app is the record.

**Teach the village to report facts, not theories.** The most useful report is
the dullest one: a white van, no markings, parked outside the recreation ground
for forty minutes at about half past two, drove off towards the main road. The
least useful one names somebody. If you say this often enough in the first three
months, it sets the tone for good. A line in the newsletter — "please say what
you saw, not who you think it was" — does most of the work.

**Use the weekly summary to brief your PCSO.** Every Sunday morning the app puts
together the week's reports for your village and flags anything that looks like
a pattern — several similar reports close together in space and time. Skim it,
and if there is a pattern in it, forward the period report to your police
contact on the Monday. Regular, boring, well-formatted contact with a named
officer is worth far more than an urgent email once a year.

**Check the queue twice a day if auto-approve is off.** Morning and evening is
plenty. A report that sits for two days is a resident who concludes that nobody
is reading, and they do not file the next one. If you cannot manage twice a day,
that is a good reason to appoint a second coordinator — or to turn auto-approve
on deliberately, having read the warnings, rather than leaving reports to
languish.

**Ask your police contact about the privacy level.** They may have a view, and
if they do it is better informed than a guess. In the absence of one, leave it
on the standard blur.

**Look at the audit trail occasionally.** Not because you suspect anybody, but
because a record nobody ever reads is a record that is not doing its job. Once a
quarter is enough, and it is a reasonable thing to mention in a council report:
"the trail shows four occasions on which a coordinator viewed an original
report, all in connection with rejections".

**Do not let it become the village's emergency number.** Say it at the start,
say it in the newsletter, and reject reports that should have been 999 calls
with a note that says so. The app is a record and a warning system, not a route
to the police.

**Say who you are.** Villages engage with a named person, not an app. Put your
name and the fact that you coordinate VillageWatch in the parish newsletter, so
residents know who to talk to when they are unsure about reporting something.

## Getting help

**Email info@yakasista.com.** That address reaches the people who run the
service. There is no ticketing system and no telephone queue.

**Reporting a technical problem.** Tell us what you were trying to do, what
happened instead, and roughly when. If a screen showed an error, say what it
said — but do not send a screenshot of anything containing report text, resident
names or original wording. If we need to see the data, we will ask, and we will
tell you how to send it safely. Your village name and the report reference (the
short code beginning VW) are enough for us to find almost anything.

**Requesting a feature.** Genuinely welcome, and the requests that come from
coordinators actually running villages are the ones that get built. Tell us what
you are trying to achieve rather than what button you want, and tell us how
often it comes up. "I spend twenty minutes every Monday retyping the week's
reports into an email for our PCSO" is a much better request than "please add an
export button", and it is the kind that gets a better answer than the one you
asked for.

**Anything urgent** — a data breach, a letter from the Information
Commissioner, a resident being harassed through the app — email us the same day
and say "urgent" in the subject line.

**In an emergency, call 999.** VillageWatch is not monitored, is not a route to
the police, and nobody is watching the queue at three in the morning. For
non-urgent police matters, 101 or your force's website.
